shipodds

When should a new agent version replace the current one? Eleven promotion gates, plus a futility stop and two reference rules, replayed on real recorded agent trials. Each is scored on false promotions, power and cost.

43.9%identical copies promoted by "higher mean wins" with one trial per task
2.4%the same trials and cost, read with a stratified test (cmh_1)
56.4%of a full evaluation's cost for the sequential gate with a futility stop

The problem

A gate can be wrong in two ways

A team that runs agents in production changes them constantly: a new model, a reworded prompt, a new policy. Each change goes through a promotion gate. It runs the candidate and the incumbent on an evaluation suite, then promotes or holds.

Agent runs are noisy. The same agent passes a task once and fails it the next time, and every run costs money. So the gate can promote a candidate that is not better, or it can spend far more runs than the decision needed. This project measures both on recorded runs instead of arguing about them.

How it works

Replay every rule on the recorded world

The data is the tau2-bench paper's published runs: four models, three customer-service domains plus setup variants, four trials of every task, with reward and dollar cost. That makes 26 configs and 52 candidate and incumbent pairs, because every comparison is counted in both directions. The pairs also include identical copies, where promoting is always wrong.

The rules fall in four groups. The mean gate promotes when the candidate's mean is higher. The tests (a task bootstrap and a stratified Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test, one stratum per task) promote only when the difference is clear, either one-shot or at fixed n of 1, 2 or 4 trials. Sequential versions look after each round with Pocock or O'Brien-Fleming boundaries, and obf_futility_* adds a stop that holds when the candidate is not ahead. Reference rules are always_hold, which is the floor, and naive_peek, the fixed test checked after every round with no correction.

Read across one row. mean_1 is cheap and powerful, and it promotes an identical copy 43.9% of the time. cmh_1 costs the same and promotes copies 2.4% of the time, at a power of only 38.6%. always_hold shows what perfect safety with no use looks like.

Two effects

A test stops false promotions. Sequential stopping saves money.

  • Test versus mean gate, at equal cost. mean_1 and cmh_1 see exactly the same trials. Only the way they read noise differs, and that takes false promotions from 43.9% to 2.4%.
  • Sequential versus fixed, at equal error. sequential_obf promotes 69.2% of better candidates against 70.2% for cmh_4, at 71.5% of the cost. But when the change does nothing, it rarely stops early and costs 99.4%.
  • A futility stop saves trials but holds good candidates. obf_futility_1.0 holds early when the candidate is not ahead. That cuts the cost when the change does nothing, but it also holds 24.0% of truly better candidates that the full test would have kept testing. It promotes 2.0% of identical copies, 67.1% of better candidates, and costs 56.4% on real pairs and 56.2% on identical copies.
  • Safety costs power. Every safe rule misses most small gains. See the table of power by true difference in the README.

Limitations

How small this is

  • Only 26 distinct comparisons. Each is counted in both directions, and some configs appear in several. Group rates are averages over a few dozen comparisons, not independent measurements.
  • The v1 rule was picked in sample, with caps set after a prototype run on the same data.
  • The three domain folds are exploratory post-selection checks and carry no intervals.
  • The recorded runs are the world, with at most four trials per task. Real noise may be larger. Error rates are relative to this simulated world, not to agents' true quality.
  • 2025 models and prices, customer-service domains, and the paper's June 2025 task sets. Nothing here speaks to current agents.
  • The statistical gates promote a worse candidate rarely, not never. The rates round to zero in the tables.

Technical details

Method, results and data

Method

Simulated trials, seeded

A new trial is one of the task's recorded trials, drawn at random, so the recorded runs are the world and the true difference is known. Each gate is replayed 2,000 times per pair. Fixed seeds make two runs byte-identical.

The design notes, with the seeds and the selection procedure, are in docs/DESIGN.md. The full tables are in the README.

The warning

Checking the numbers every round looks cheaper and stronger

Naive peeking promotes 6.1% of identical copies. That is naive_peek, the fixed test checked after every round with no correction, which is what a team does when it just keeps an eye on the numbers. It also looks good on the other two axes, with a power of 72.3% and a cost of 53.7%. It looks that way only because it is allowed more errors. The sequential boundaries exist to spend the error budget across looks.

What v2 changed

The headline was reworded, the evidence was widened

v1 read as "the sequential gate lowers false promotions at lower cost". The data support two separate claims instead, shown on the Overview. v2 added the futility rules, the two reference rules (always_hold, naive_peek), the held-out folds, a power-by-difference table, a smoothed-noise world and a grid of caps. The v1 results file stays byte-identical.

The futility grid was later extended with two larger thresholds, because the first grid stopped at the picked one. The pick, obf_futility_1.0, now sits at an interior point of the grid, and the larger thresholds lose more power than the slack allows.
